\begin{abstract}
Models of the refractive index of air are used to compensate environmentally driven phase noise in free-space optical paths, but their accuracy at a given wavelength is rarely verified directly. We measure the refractive index of air at \SI{1762}{\nano\meter}, the $^{138}\mathrm{Ba}^+$ clock transition, with a quantum-referenced architecture that combines the ratio of interference-fringe counts of two atom-referenced lasers over a common \SI{4.2}{\meter} air path with one environmental sensor feeding the reference, the regression, and the model prediction. From \num{145784} synchronized observations collected over eight months, we obtain the temperature, humidity, and pressure coefficients $\alpha_T = \SI{-8.8474e-7}{\per\kelvin}$, $\alpha_H = \SI{-1.3152e-8}{\per\percent}$, and $\alpha_P = \SI{+2.5949e-7}{\per\hecto\pascal}$. Compared with Mathar predictions on the identical conditions, the temperature and pressure coefficients agree to within \SI{0.35}{\percent} and \SI{0.93}{\percent} (consistency checks, not independent calibrations, since the comparison shares the environmental inputs), while the humidity coefficient is \SI{24.8}{\percent} weaker and its difference is not resolved: the significance depends strongly on the temporal-dependence treatment, and the in-domain comparison does not resolve a difference. A wavelength-dependent static offset of \SI{4.16}{\radian\per\meter} dominates the residual; after a baseline-offset correction the empirical model improves the residual phase error over Mathar by about \SI{5}{\percent} in-sample. The measurement architecture provides a quantum-referenced route to measuring the refractive index of air, and the dataset provides a reference for related measurements and for independent reanalysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Optical interconnects between remote trapped-ion quantum nodes require phase-stable links for protocols that depend on the first-order coherence of the optical fields, such as free-space optical clock comparisons \cite{giorgetta2013optical,shen2022free} and coherent detection of transmitted fields. Photon-counting protocols based on post-selection, such as atomic-ensemble quantum memories \cite{duan2001long} and Barrett-Kok-type schemes \cite{barrett2005efficient}, are insensitive to the common optical phase accumulated along the link and therefore do not require phase-stable optical paths. For $^{138}\mathrm{Ba}^+$ based quantum networks requiring phase-stable links, the operating wavelength is \SI{1762}{\nano\meter}, where the $6S_{1/2} \rightarrow 5D_{5/2}$ electric quadrupole transition provides the physical two-level system \cite{dietrich2009barium,arnold2020precision}. The refractive index of air along the optical path depends on temperature ($T$), humidity ($H$), and pressure ($P$), and its fluctuations translate directly into phase errors of the transmitted field.

Compensation of these phase errors requires the refractive index's environmental dependence at the operating wavelength. Atmospheric refractivity is described by broadband formulations, most commonly the Edl\'{e}n, Ciddor, and Mathar models \cite{edlen1966refractive,ciddor1996refractive,mathar2007refractive}; later work refined the Edl{\'e}n dispersion for the visible and near infrared and compared it with direct measurements of air refractivity \cite{birch1993updated,bonsch1998measurement}. These models are fitted to refractivity data over wide spectral intervals and interpolate between sparse calibration wavelengths via dispersion formulas; their accuracy at a single wavelength is therefore not guaranteed a priori. The Edl\'{e}n and Ciddor formulations are calibrated over the visible and near-infrared (\SIrange{0.4}{1.3}{\micro\meter}), with stated validity extending only to about \SI{1.7}{\micro\meter}, leaving \SI{1762}{\nano\meter} outside their calibrated range. The Mathar model extends the dispersion formalism into the mid-infrared and is the model applicable at \SI{1762}{\nano\meter}. Direct measurements of the refractive index of air have been reported at other wavelengths, for example using two-colour optical frequency combs \cite{wu2013extremely,wu2014corrigendum}; validation of the refractivity models at \SI{1762}{\nano\meter} has, to our knowledge, not been reported.

Here we report a quantum-referenced measurement architecture for the refractive index of air at \SI{1762}{\nano\meter} and its application over an eight-month campaign. Two atom-frequency-referenced lasers traverse a common \SI{4.2}{\meter} round-trip air path; the directly measured quantity is the ratio of their interference-fringe counts, and the refractive index follows from that ratio together with a Ciddor reference at \SI{780}{\nano\meter}. A single environmental sensor drives the reference, the regression, and the model prediction. The campaign yields the environmental coefficients $\partial n/\partial X$ ($X = T, H, P$), which we compare with the Mathar model evaluated on the identical observed conditions. The temperature and pressure coefficients agree with the model to within \SI{0.35}{\percent} and \SI{0.93}{\percent}; because the comparison shares the environmental inputs, these agreements are consistency checks, not independent sensor calibrations. The humidity coefficient is smaller in magnitude than the same-condition Mathar surrogate by \SI{24.8}{\percent}; the humidity coefficient difference remains unresolved: its significance is not robust across the examined dependence treatments, the full-campaign comparison involves Mathar extrapolation, and the in-domain comparison has limited discriminatory power; its interpretation is discussed below. We also report the residual analysis of the empirical model relative to the Mathar formulation and the associated residual scatter. The measurement architecture, the dataset, and the comparison protocol are described so that the comparison can be reproduced independently from the released data.

\section{Experimental Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The experimental system is organized into three functional blocks, color-coded in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}. The measurement node (blue) provides frequency-referenced laser light and records the environmental conditions at the interferometer site. The \SI{1762}{\nano\meter} probe laser is stabilized to an ultra-low-expansion (ULE) optical cavity (finesse 360,000) and referenced to the electric quadrupole transition ($6S_{1/2} \rightarrow 5D_{5/2}$) of a single trapped $^{138}\mathrm{Ba}^+$ ion, yielding short-term stability $\delta f < \SI{1}{\kilo\hertz}$ \cite{yum2017optical,arnold2020precision}. The \SI{780}{\nano\meter} reference laser is stabilized to the $^{87}$Rb D$_2$ line via Doppler-free saturated absorption spectroscopy ($\delta f < \SI{1}{\mega\hertz}$). A BME280 sensor \cite{bme280} co-located with the interferometer monitors temperature $T$, relative humidity $H$, and pressure $P$ (accuracies $\pm\SI{1.0}{\degreeCelsius}$, $\pm\SI{3}{\percent}$ RH, and $\pm\SI{1}{\hecto\pascal}$). Both lasers share a GPS-disciplined \SI{10}{\mega\hertz} timebase, which stamps every measurement for synchronization; environmental sensors were sampled at \SI{2}{\hertz}.

The interferometer node (red) is a dual-wavelength Michelson interferometer (modified LM10 design of the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) \cite{fox1999reliable}) measuring over a common round-trip optical path $L = \SI{4.2}{\meter}$ (total propagation). The directly measured quantity is the ratio $R(t)$ of the interference fringe counts accumulated at the two wavelengths, recorded by a frequency counter over \SIrange{10}{20}{\second} per sample. Because both beams traverse the same air path, vibrations and drifts common to the two channels cancel in $R(t)$, isolating the differential refractivity of the air. The refractive index at \SI{1762}{\nano\meter} is a derived quantity,

\begin{equation}
n_{1762}(t) = \frac{f_{780}}{f_{1762}}\; \frac{n_{780}\big(T(t),H(t),P(t)\big)}{R(t)},
\label{eqn:wavemeter}
\end{equation}

where $f_{780}$ and $f_{1762}$ are the optical frequencies of the two channels, both fixed by atomic references (the $^{87}$Rb D$_2$ line and the $^{138}\mathrm{Ba}^+$ quadrupole transition, respectively), and $n_{780}$ is obtained from the Ciddor model \cite{ciddor1996refractive}, using the reference implementation provided by NIST \cite{nist_refractiveindex}, evaluated at the co-located sensor readings.

Equation~(\ref{eqn:wavemeter}) makes explicit what is measured and what is assumed. The directly measured quantity is the fringe-count ratio $R(t)$; the inferred $n_{1762}$ is differential, anchored to the Ciddor model at \SI{780}{\nano\meter} \cite{ciddor1996refractive}, whose input in turn is provided by the co-located BME280 sensor. The inferred $n_{1762}$ therefore inherits any constant offset of the 780~nm anchor as an additive constant; this appears in our data as the static offset discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}. The environmental coefficients $\partial n/\partial X$, which are the quantities of interest here, are unaffected by such a constant because it is absorbed by the fitted intercept. The same sensor therefore supplies three branches of the analysis, namely the Ciddor reference $n_{780}(T,H,P)$, the regression predictors, and the Mathar predictions evaluated at the same conditions, so that any perturbation of the sensor calibration propagates consistently through all three. The inferred $n_{1762}$ is the path-averaged refractivity over the \SI{4.2}{\meter} round-trip, whereas $T$, $H$, and $P$ are single-point readings at one end of the path; this spatial mismatch is the dominant contribution to the residual scatter (Supplemental Material). The interferometer and the sensor sit in a non-temperature-stabilized building corridor (temperature \SIrange{17.5}{34.6}{\degreeCelsius}, relative humidity \SIrange{18.9}{45.3}{\percent}, pressure \SIrange{964.6}{1006.8}{\hecto\pascal} over the campaign), while the quantum frequency references are operated in a temperature-stabilized laboratory.

Samples recorded while either laser was unlocked were identified by fringe-count ratios inconsistent with locked operation and were removed during preprocessing. This retention criterion, together with the segment structure of the campaign (104 segments between January and August 2025, duty cycle near 15\%, see Supplemental Material), defines the dataset of \num{145784} synchronized measurements \cite{wu_2026_18800166}.

The refractive index at \SI{1762}{\nano\meter} is modeled as a linear function of the environmental variables,

\begin{equation}
n(T,H,P) = n_0 + \alpha_T T + \alpha_H H + \alpha_P P
\end{equation}

where $n_0$ is a fitted intercept and $\alpha_j$ are the environmental coefficients determined by multivariate ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression on the synchronized time series. Statistical robustness is validated through a suite of diagnostics detailed in Supplemental Material, with uncertain quantities reported as distributions rather than as a single combined value \cite{jcgm101}. These include moving-block bootstrap resampling over physical-time blocks whose duration is varied from \SI{0.6}{\hour} to \SI{48}{\hour}, with the fraction of observations covered by the block pool reported for each duration ($B = 400$ per duration in the duration scan; $B = 5000$ for the coefficient standard errors) \cite{kunsch1989jackknife,politis1994stationary}, and heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors with a Bartlett kernel \cite{newey1987hypothesis,andrews1991heteroskedasticity}. A single nominal block length is not adopted as a decision rule: the residual autocorrelation retains a diurnal component, so no single decorrelation scale describes the full dependence, and the block-duration sensitivity of the standard errors is reported instead of one nominal value. The within-campaign consistency of the linear surrogate is assessed with a blocked cross-validation \cite{roberts2017cross,bergmeir2012use}: the data are divided into contiguous blocks assigned to five folds, and within each fold the surrogate is fitted on the training blocks and evaluated on the held-out blocks, with identical folds and no global preprocessing before the split (Supplemental Material). The blocks are assigned to folds across the whole campaign rather than held out in time, so this is an internal consistency check and not a temporally separated validation.

\section{Results and Validation}
\label{sec:results}

The synchronized dataset of the derived refractive index and the related environmental parameters, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}, spans eight months (January to August 2025) as a series of 104 observation segments with an overall duty cycle near 15\%. The mean temperature, relative humidity, and pressure over the retained observations are \SI{28.9}{\degreeCelsius}, \SI{30.2}{\percent}, and \SI{986.4}{\hecto\pascal}, with standard deviations of \SI{3.4}{\degreeCelsius}, \SI{3.9}{\percent}, and \SI{4.5}{\hecto\pascal}, respectively; a time-weighted average is not meaningful here because the 111-day gap between the January-February block and the June-August body of the campaign would dominate the calendar-duration weights (see Supplemental Material). Details of the temporal coverage are provided in the Supplemental Material.

The refractive index time series, derived from the measured fringe-count ratio via Eq.~(\ref{eqn:wavemeter}), tracks the environmental fluctuations with clear diurnal periodicity. Data gaps, visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}, result from ion reloading cycles and occasional interferometer realignment; all valid time points from the full dataset (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) are used for the regression analysis.

Multivariate regression following the linear model defined in Section~\ref{sec:methods} yields the environmental coefficients listed in Table~\ref{tab:coefficients}. To place the comparison with the Mathar model on equal footing, we evaluate the Mathar formulation at the identical $(T,H,P)$ rows of the campaign and fit the same linear surrogate model to both datasets, as detailed in Supplemental Material. The temperature and pressure coefficients agree with this same-condition Mathar surrogate within \SI{0.35}{\percent} and \SI{0.93}{\percent}, respectively; because the comparison shares the environmental inputs, these agreements are consistency checks, not independent sensor calibrations. In contrast, the humidity coefficient is smaller in magnitude than the same-condition Mathar surrogate by \SI{24.8}{\percent}. The humidity coefficient difference remains unresolved: its significance is not robust across the examined dependence treatments, the full-campaign comparison involves Mathar extrapolation, and the in-domain comparison has limited discriminatory power. The sampled joint gain-offset sensitivities displace it by $-0.58\times10^{-9}$ to $+1.02\times10^{-9}$ ($-13.3\%$ to $+23.6\%$ of its value), with the temperature and pressure channels corresponding to $-34\%$ to $+26\%$ and $-5\%$ to $+4\%$, respectively. These are sampled sensitivities over the specification-justified region, not certified global bounds; the linearised surrogate is $0.92\times10^{-9}$ for humidity and $1.92\times10^{-9}$ for temperature. The difference is therefore reported as an unresolved systematic, compatible with the unresolved slow drift of the fringe-ratio anchor.

Table~\ref{tab:phase_shift} lists the resulting phase-shift sensitivities per unit environmental change. The residual analysis of the two models is as follows. The empirical model achieves a residual standard deviation $\sigma_n = 1.84 \times 10^{-7}$, corresponding to a coefficient of determination $R^2 = 0.996$. The Mathar formulation evaluated at the same wavelength produces a root-mean-square residual error of $1.18 \times 10^{-6}$, dominated by a static offset of \num{1.166e-6} (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}(b)). Since this offset is constant over the campaign, subtracting it leaves a residual standard deviation of \num{1.93e-7}. On this offset-subtracted basis, the empirical model improves the residual phase error over the Mathar model by \SI{4.9}{\percent} (amplitude) and \SI{9.5}{\percent} (variance) in the full-campaign fit, in which the empirical coefficients and the static offset are estimated on the same data that are used for the evaluation. This comparison is summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}. The in-sample residual of \num{1.84e-7} is consistent with the within-campaign blocked cross-validation residual of $(1.84 \pm 0.02) \times 10^{-7}$ for the empirical surrogate (Supplemental Material); because the cross-validation blocks are interleaved across the campaign rather than separated in time, this supports internal consistency but is not a temporally held-out validation. A temporally separated evaluation of the same offset-fair comparison, in which the coefficients and the static offset are estimated on a training interval and applied to a later held-out interval, is reported in the Supplemental Material; the improvement does not persist there, and we interpret the full-campaign value as an in-sample difference rather than as a prediction of compensation performance on other intervals or time spans.

A budget of the single-epoch refractive-index determination is provided in Supplemental Material. The dominant term is the residual scatter of the regression ($\delta n = 1.84 \times 10^{-7}$, $1\sigma$), which reflects the spatial and temporal mismatch between the environmental sensor and the optical path and is not resolved into independent contributions. We report these terms separately and do not quote a combined absolute-index uncertainty; the \SI{1762}{\nano\meter} quantum frequency reference contributes below $10^{-12}$, while the \SI{780}{\nano\meter} reference contributes a bound below $2.6 \times 10^{-9}$.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This work reports a quantum-referenced measurement architecture for the refractive index of air at \SI{1762}{\nano\meter}, the $^{138}\mathrm{Ba}^+$ clock transition, applied over an eight-month campaign. The dataset of \num{145784} synchronized measurements yields the environmental coefficients $\partial n/\partial X$ for temperature, relative humidity, and pressure reported in Table~\ref{tab:coefficients}, together with their bootstrap standard errors. The residual analysis of the empirical model relative to the Mathar formulation is reported in the Results section.

The comparison with the Mathar model is made on identical observed conditions, so that both fits share the same design matrix (Results). Because the comparison shares the environmental inputs, the temperature and pressure agreements are consistency checks rather than independent sensor calibrations.

The humidity coefficient difference remains unresolved (Results): the in-domain comparison has limited discriminatory power because the retained observations in the Mathar validity domain ($T \in [10,25]\,\si{\degreeCelsius}$) span only \SI{4.6}{\kelvin}. Two checks bear on its interpretation. First, the direction of the difference is opposite to the sign expected from anomalous dispersion near a water-vapor absorption line: such a response would increase the measured magnitude relative to the broadband value, whereas the measured magnitude is smaller. Second, the magnitude of the difference is not spanned by the gain uncertainty of the BME280 humidity sensor when the three environmental channels ($T$, $H$, $P$) are propagated consistently: the \SI{4}{\percent} accuracy bound over the campaign temperature span (\SI{17.1}{\kelvin}) corresponds to a worst-case single-channel gain deviation of 30.3\%, but propagating the gain and offset bounds consistently through all three channels reduces the sampled sensitivity well below the single-branch endpoint estimate of $3.99 \times 10^{-9}$ (Results and Supplemental Material). These are sampled sensitivities and not certified global bounds, and they do not account for the sign of the difference. The residual difference remains compatible with the unresolved slow drift of the fringe-ratio anchor, whose data-driven injection ($4.4 \times 10^{-9}$) can account for the whole difference (see Supplemental Material). On the basis of these checks, the humidity difference is reported as an unresolved systematic.

Several limitations of this work follow from the measurement configuration and are described in Supplemental Material. The environmental parameters were sampled by a single BME280 sensor at \SI{2}{\hertz}, so the residual reflects the spatial and temporal mismatch between that sensor and the \SI{4.2}{\meter} optical path, and turbulent fluctuations are not resolved. The humidity channel remains unresolved because no traceable humidity calibration was available; a gain systematic at the level of the sensor specification cannot be excluded, although the sampled joint gain-offset sensitivities do not span the difference (see above and Supplemental Material). The laser-lock retention window (fringe-count ratio in $[2.2584867,\,3]$) has a lower bound that coincides with the smallest retained value, so it is data-defined and could in principle remove valid observations near the boundary; the rationale for this bound is not documented. Trimming the lowest-ratio retained observations changes the humidity coefficient by less than its statistical standard error and the ratio is essentially uncorrelated with the environmental state, which establishes robustness to further rejection within the retained sample; it does not exclude a bias introduced by the original acceptance cut (Supplemental Material). The acquisition model is used as an effective description of the accumulated fringe counts: the scan and counter-gate timing, and the synchronisation between the counter and the environmental sensors (a nearest-neighbour merge with a median matching distance of \SI{0.4}{\second}, a $95$th percentile of \SI{4.3}{\second} and a maximum of \SI{458}{\second}), are not instrumented in the data release. CO$_2$ variations were not tracked. These limitations do not affect the reported comparisons between the measured coefficients and the Mathar surrogate, which use identical environmental rows for both fits; they set the accuracy with which the coefficients themselves are determined, as quantified in Supplemental Material.

In summary, the work comprises a measurement architecture in which a single trapped ion sets the optical frequency of an interferometric refractometry channel over a \SI{4.2}{\meter} ambient air path; a multi-month, high-cadence dataset of \num{145784} synchronized measurements of refractive index and co-located environmental parameters; a comparison protocol that evaluates the Mathar model on the identical observed conditions and fits both the measurement and the model with the same linear surrogate; and an offset-fair, in-sample assessment in which a single baseline calibration removes the static offset and the empirical model reduces the residual scatter by approximately \SI{5}{\percent} relative to the debiased Mathar model. The comparison also identifies two identifiability limitations of this dataset: the unresolved slow drift of the fringe-ratio anchor and the limited coverage of the Mathar validity domain (approximately \SI{92}{\percent} of the retained observations lie above \SI{25}{\degreeCelsius}, the upper edge of that domain).
